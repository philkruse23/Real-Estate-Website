> “Travelers are pickier and booking later.”

That observation from Visit Florida President and CEO Bryan Griffin may be one of the most important signals for Florida’s tourism industry in 2026.

Florida is not facing a tourism collapse. The state welcomed an estimated 73.5 million visitors during the first half of 2026, keeping visitation relatively close to the record pace established in recent years.

But underneath those large numbers, a meaningful change appears to be taking place.

Consumers are watching their spending more carefully. Travelers who flocked to Florida earlier in the decade are looking for something different. Canadian visitation has weakened substantially. And people are waiting longer before committing to trips.

Visit Florida’s response is equally significant: instead of relying primarily on major seasonal campaigns, the state is shifting toward an always-on tourism marketing strategy while placing greater emphasis on rural communities and destinations beyond Florida’s best-known tourism centers.

That shift could reshape not only how Florida markets itself, but also which communities benefit from tourism spending.

## Florida Tourism Is Slowing, But the Bigger Story Is Changing Consumer Behavior

Florida entered this period from an unusually strong position.

The state recorded 143.3 million visitors in 2025, according to figures cited by Visit Florida. Approximately 74.5 million of those visitors arrived during the first half of that year.

During the first six months of 2026, visitation declined to roughly 73.5 million.

A 1.4% decline is relatively modest, particularly after several years of exceptionally strong tourism.

What may matter more is why the market is softening.

Griffin pointed to two forces that tourism destinations across the country are increasingly watching closely.

Consumers are becoming more cautious about discretionary spending, and travelers have more choices.

Florida benefited tremendously during the period following the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly as the state reopened earlier than some competing destinations.

But travelers who repeatedly visited Florida during that period may now be ready to explore somewhere else.

That creates a classic destination-marketing challenge: familiarity is valuable, but familiarity can also increase competition from destinations offering something new.

Florida is not simply competing against other beach destinations. It is competing for limited household travel budgets against nearly every experience a consumer might choose.

## Higher Costs Are Changing How Americans Travel

The domestic tourism market is especially important because domestic visitors account for nearly 92% of Florida tourism.

That concentration means even relatively small changes in American consumer spending can have significant consequences for Florida hotels, restaurants, attractions and local tourism economies.

As Griffin explained, travelers are “tightening their belts.”

That phrase captures one of the defining tourism trends of the moment.

Consumers have not necessarily stopped traveling. Instead, many are becoming more selective about:

- Where they travel
- How long they stay
- Which hotels they book
- How much they spend at the destination
- How far in advance they make reservations
- Whether the trip feels worth the total cost

That final consideration is particularly important.

Destinations increasingly have to demonstrate value before consumers commit.

For Florida, tourism marketing is therefore not simply about reminding people that beaches, theme parks and sunshine exist.

The challenge is convincing travelers that a Florida vacation deserves a larger share of an increasingly scrutinized household budget.

## Visit Florida Is Moving Toward Always-On Tourism Marketing

One of the biggest strategic changes described by Griffin is Visit Florida’s move away from isolated seasonal campaigns.

Previously, marketing efforts might have centered around specific campaigns promoting winter travel, family vacations, outdoor experiences or nature.

Now Visit Florida is creating what Griffin described as a connected, always-on system.

The strategy reflects changing booking behavior.

### Why Year-Round Tourism Marketing Matters

Traditional tourism advertising often assumes consumers follow relatively predictable planning cycles.

Families might book summer vacations months in advance. Snowbirds might begin thinking about winter travel at a particular time of year.

But digital travel planning has disrupted those patterns.

Consumers can research a destination one evening, compare airfare the next day and book a hotel days or sometimes hours before traveling.

When travelers book later, tourism organizations cannot afford long periods when their destinations disappear from consumers’ feeds, search results and advertising environments.

An always-on strategy helps Florida remain visible throughout that decision-making process.

Rather than trying to create demand only during specific campaign windows, Florida can continuously reinforce one message:

Florida should remain on the traveler’s shortlist.

## Rural Florida Could Be One of the Biggest Beneficiaries

Another element of Visit Florida’s strategy deserves attention: its emphasis on rural communities.

Florida tourism is often associated with a relatively small group of globally recognized destinations.

Orlando.

Miami.

The Florida Keys.

Tampa Bay.

Fort Lauderdale.

Palm Beach.

Those destinations will continue to be major tourism engines.

But promoting smaller communities could give Florida something particularly valuable at a time when consumers are looking for new experiences.

Novelty.

### Rural Tourism Expands the Florida Travel Story

Someone who has already visited Florida several times may not need another advertisement showing the same destinations they already know.

But that traveler might respond to a Florida experience they have never considered.

That could include:

- Small historic towns
- Natural springs
- State parks
- Fishing communities
- Agricultural experiences
- Wildlife tourism
- Local festivals
- Hiking and paddling destinations
- Ecotourism
- Rural food and cultural experiences

Promoting those destinations can accomplish several things simultaneously.

It gives repeat visitors a reason to return. It distributes tourism spending across more communities. And it helps Florida compete with destinations that market themselves around authenticity, nature and less-crowded experiences.

For rural Florida communities, that creates a potentially significant economic development opportunity.

Tourism dollars do not stop at hotels.

Visitors spend money at restaurants, gas stations, retail businesses, outfitters, attractions and other locally owned businesses.

Even modest increases in visitation can therefore have an outsized impact in smaller communities.

## Canadian Travel to Florida Remains a Major Challenge

Florida’s international tourism picture is more complicated.

Canadian travelers have historically represented an important market for the state, particularly during winter.

But visitation has weakened considerably.

Canadian travel to Florida fell nearly 7% in 2025 compared with 2024. During the first half of 2026, approximately 1.68 million Canadian travelers visited Florida, representing a 13.9% decline from the same period in 2025.

Those are significant declines.

The shift also appears to reflect a broader change in Canadian travel behavior toward the United States.

Statistics Canada reported that U.S. return border crossings by Canadian residents remained depressed in early 2026, suggesting that the change is not exclusively a Florida problem.

Trade disputes, tariffs and political rhetoric have contributed to strained attitudes toward U.S. travel.

Yet Visit Florida is not abandoning the market.

Griffin said the organization continues to invest in Canada and the United Kingdom as core international markets.

That strategy may prove valuable over the longer term.

Tourism organizations cannot control international politics. They can, however, maintain brand awareness so that when consumer sentiment changes, their destination remains familiar.

There is also an important nuance in Florida’s Canadian numbers.

Even as overall Canadian travel to the United States declined, Griffin said Florida increased its share of Canadian U.S. travel by three percentage points last year.

In other words, Florida may be losing Canadian visitors while simultaneously outperforming competing American destinations within a shrinking market.

## Florida Tourism Marketing Is Becoming a Battle for Attention

The visitor numbers tell one story.

Consumer behavior tells another.

The second may ultimately matter more.

Florida’s tourism industry is entering an environment where simply being famous is no longer enough.

Travelers have access to almost unlimited destination inspiration through TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, travel blogs, airline deals and booking platforms.

A consumer considering Florida can discover a beach in Mexico, a mountain town in North Carolina, a Caribbean resort or an inexpensive European flight within minutes.

That creates enormous competition for attention.

Visit Florida’s year-round strategy reflects that reality.

The state wants to remain present while consumers move through what has become an increasingly unpredictable travel-planning process.

## What Rural Communities Can Do With This Opportunity

Statewide tourism marketing can generate awareness, but communities still have to convert that awareness into actual visits.

For rural destinations, several priorities stand out.

First, communities should identify the experiences that genuinely differentiate them rather than trying to imitate larger tourism destinations.

Second, those experiences should be easy to discover online.

Travelers should quickly be able to answer basic questions such as:

- What can visitors do there?
- Where should they stay?
- Where should they eat?
- What makes the destination different?
- Can visitors build a weekend itinerary?
- How far is it from a larger city or airport?

Third, tourism organizations can benefit from creating itineraries instead of simply listing attractions.

Travelers do not necessarily think in tourism-industry categories. They think about experiences.

A well-designed “48 Hours in Rural Florida” itinerary can sometimes be more persuasive than twenty individual attraction pages.

Finally, local tourism organizations should make sure businesses understand the opportunity.

Marketing may bring someone into a community once.

The quality of the experience determines whether that visitor recommends it, returns or stays another night.

## The Next Phase of Florida Tourism Will Be About Value and Discovery

Florida still possesses something most destinations would like to have: enormous tourism awareness.

The challenge now is converting that awareness into trips during a period when consumers are questioning nearly every discretionary purchase.

That makes Visit Florida’s strategic shift noteworthy.

Year-round marketing addresses later booking behavior.

Promoting rural destinations gives repeat visitors something new to discover.

Maintaining international investment protects Florida’s position in markets that could eventually rebound.

And emphasizing value becomes increasingly important as household budgets remain under pressure.

The headline may be that Florida tourism declined slightly during the first half of 2026.

But the more consequential story is that the tourism playbook itself is changing.

Travelers are becoming more selective.

Destinations are competing harder.

And communities that previously sat outside Florida’s traditional tourism spotlight may suddenly have an opportunity to become part of the state’s next chapter of growth.

For tourism organizations, economic development groups and rural Florida communities, this is a good time to examine their destinations through a traveler’s eyes. They can search for their communities, review what potential visitors actually find and determine whether their strongest experiences are visible enough to turn curiosity into a trip.

**What will matter most to Florida tourism over the next few years: affordability, international visitation, or getting travelers to discover destinations beyond the state’s traditional tourism centers?**
